Speedrunning: The art of "play game fast"
This visualisation explores the speedrunning community
and the trends behind the games that form it.

Which Countries Love Speedrunning?

The United States dominates with over 400,000 speedruns, driven by its large population, streaming culture, and American-developed games. Japan ranks second, benefiting from being home to Nintendo, Sega, and other major developers.

Europe and Canada show solid participation. Antarctica's data is likely inaccurate due to speedrunners listing joke nationalities. The map reveals speedrunning as a predominantly Western and Japanese phenomenon.

Ocarina of Time: A Category for Everyone

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, one of my favorite games, offers speedrunners multiple ways to compete. Any% allows all glitches for the fastest completion. Any% Glitchless removes major skips. No Wrong Warp bans a specific teleportation glitch that skips large game portions. 100% requires completing all objectives.

Popularity shows runner interest in each category. World Records displays completion times, with Any% dramatically shorter due to glitch exploitation. Competition reveals how close the top runners are: Any% shows an incredibly tight 0.2-second gap between first and third place. Freshness indicates how recently records were broken, indicating the lasting popularity of a classic game.

Category Clustering in Speedrunning

These violin plots reveal the distribution of world record times across different speedrun categories, showing where most runners' times fall. The y-axis uses a logarithmic scale, meaning each step up represents times 10 times longer, helping visualise the massive range from sub-minute runs to multi-hour marathons.

Any% shows extreme concentration at the lower end with a dramatic spike at the top, reflecting both heavily optimised short runs and a few outlier games with longer completion times. Any% Glitchless displays a more balanced distribution, as removing major skips naturally spreads times across a wider range. The 100% category's broad middle section shows how completionist runs vary significantly between games. Any% No Major Glitches sits between the extremes, allowing minor tricks while preventing game-breaking shortcuts.

The narrow spikes at the bottom of each plot represent the fastest possible times, where optimization has pushed records to their theoretical limits.

Do Awards Drive Speedrunning?

Awards verus Speedrunning Popularity

This strip plot examines whether The Game Awards recognition correlates with speedrunning activity for games released between 2014-2019. The y-axis uses a logarithmic scale, where each step represents a tenfold increase in total speedruns.

The "Other" category naturally dominates by sheer volume, as most games neither win nor receive nominations. However, a clear trend emerges: award-winning games cluster higher on the speedrun count axis. Winners average around 1,000-3,000 runs, while nominated games show moderate activity in the 100-1,000 range. Most non-awarded games sit around approximately 150-200 total runs.

This correlation likely works both ways. Popular games attract both speedrunners and award voters, while award recognition can subsequently boost a game's speedrunning community. The outliers are particularly interesting: a few non-awarded games punch well above their weight in speedrun popularity, suggesting cult followings independent of critical acclaim.

What Genres are Most Popular?

This stacked area chart tracks game releases by genre from 1980 to 2025, revealing dramatic shifts in the industry. The massive peak around 2015-2020 represents the indie game explosion, where digital distribution platforms like Steam democratised game development and publishing.

RPGs (orange) and Adventure games (blue) dominate the upper layers, reflecting their enduring popularity and the rise of indie RPG makers. Action games maintain steady presence throughout. Platformers (teal) show interesting waves, peaking during the 8-bit and 16-bit eras, declining in the 3D transition, then resurging with indie development.

How Game Depth Affects Quality

Game Depth Across Genres

This dumbbell chart explores the relationship between game length and review scores across genres.

The review score filters reveal interesting patterns. When viewing all games, RPGs show the largest gap between main story and completionist playtimes, reflecting their abundance of side quests and collectibles. However, filtering for highly-rated games (85+) reveals that well-received titles across all genres tend to have more substantial optional content. Conversely, poorly-rated games (below 15) show minimal difference between main story and completionist times, suggesting that lack of depth may contribute to lower scores.

Horror and Platformer genres consistently show the smallest gaps, indicating these genres prioritise focused, streamlined experiences regardless of review scores.

How much Bang for your Buck?

This heatmap reveals where games cluster in terms of length and review scores. The bright hotspot in the lower-left corner tells a clear story: most games are short (0-10 hours) and score in the 70-80 range. This represents the well-like but not groundbreaking category that dominates the market.

Filtering by genre reveals distinct patterns. RPGs spread across longer hour ranges with consistently high scores, reflecting the genre's emphasis on depth. Horror games cluster tightly around short playtimes, prioritising focused experiences over length. Platformers show similar brevity but with wider score variation.

The darkness above 90 hours is telling. Very few games justify extreme length, and those that do tend to be RPGs. This suggests that most developers understand the value of respecting player time rather than padding content arbitrarily.
